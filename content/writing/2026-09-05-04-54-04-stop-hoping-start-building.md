If you read my last post, you already know the point I was trying to drive home. My point, exactly, is that fully funded Master’s and PhD opportunities abroad are real, and the path to them is learnable. But I also know something else, because I have been there and I’ve mentored people through it too: belief alone doesn’t move an application. Desire is the spark, yes, but structure is the fuel. So consider this the practical follow-up to that mindset shift. In the first post, we talked about the “project mindset.” In this one, I’m giving you the actual checklist, the sequence of moves that turns “I want this” into “I submitted it,” and eventually into “I got in.”  

First, start with desire, but don’t let it stay as vibes. Write down what you want in one sentence. What program or course are you interested in continuing your study in? What level (Master’s or PhD) do you intend to apply for, and what is the kind of future you want to build toward? Then write down the “why” in one paragraph for yourself. Because the truth is, scholarships are not awarded to the loudest dreamers. They go to people who can explain their direction clearly and back it up with evidence. This is where you move from general ambition to a specific target. If you can’t clearly say what you want and why, everything else you do will feel scattered, and your documents will look scattered too.  

After thinking critically about your interest, or even while doing that, you will need to handle the foundation documents that stop people at the gate. Get your international passport early. If you are reading this and truly interested in studying abroad, you should already have your international passport. Not next month, not when you “start applying,” now. A passport is not a travel document in this context; it is an access key. The same way you wouldn’t apply for a job without a valid ID, you shouldn’t be chasing international opportunities while your passport is a future plan. And while you’re doing that, request your undergraduate transcript from your school. This is another silent delay that ruins timelines. Some schools take weeks. Some will frustrate you. Some will demand follow-up. Start early so you’re not begging when deadlines are close. A lot of Nigerian universities are improving their transcript processing times, but so many universities are still terrible. To request your transcript, you do not need anyone to do it for you. You can do it by yourself. You do not need to pay anyone for anything, either. If you graduated from the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, I made a detailed video some years back on how to request your transcript by yourself. Let me know in the comments section if you need it.  

Next comes the part people love to overdo: exams. TOEFL, IELTS, GRE- yes, they matter in certain cases, but don’t let them become your whole identity. Here’s the smarter approach: check your target programs and write only what your schools actually require or what improves your chances. If you can write them, great; they expand your options. If you cannot right now, don’t let that stop your momentum. Plenty of programs waive tests or have flexible policies, but you won’t know unless you read the requirements carefully for each school or specific program. This is what I meant when I said scholarships are a process, not a rumor. You don’t guess; you verify. I have never been an advocate of English proficiency tests. In my opinion, I think there are so many schools in so many countries that I could choose from that do not require English proficiency tests when compared to the ones forcing me to. Also, you can request a waiver using an English proficiency cover letter from your Nigerian university if you find yourself at any school demanding it as a requirement. If they don’t accept it and your only choice is that university or program, you have to write the test (just know you have options). I have never written any English proficiency test, both when I went to Taiwan for my master's and even now for my PhD in the United States.  

Then, before you even think about writing big documents, go and secure the people who will speak for you. Recommendation letters are not a formality. They are one of the strongest credibility signals in your application, because they let a third party testify that you can actually do the work. So reach out to your past lecturers early, while you still have time to build the relationship properly. Don’t just ask for a letter; remind them of who you were in their class, what you worked on, and what you’re aiming for now. Give them your updated CV and a short summary of what you’re applying for. Good letters are specific. Weak letters are generic. And the difference between the two is often preparation and timing. If you are still an undergraduate, lucky you. Begin to build a good relationship with some of your professors because you will need them in the future. If you are already a graduate and you plan to apply for the 2027 application cycle, now is the time to reach out to your past lecturers.  

Now, let’s talk about schools, because this is where many people waste energy. Looking for a school is not scrolling rankings and picking vibes. You’re looking for three things: your program of interest exists there, they have funding or ways for you to fund your studies, and you actually fit what they train and fund. So build a shortlist like a strategist. Start with 10 to 20 programs. Reduce it to the ones that match your interests and requirements. Check deadlines. Check if the department offers assistantships or scholarships. Check if international students are funded. Your goal is not to apply everywhere. Your goal is to apply where you are most likely to be taken seriously.  

It is also important to consider the country. Recently, some people have asked me, “So what countries should we target now that the U.S. feels uncertain?” My answer is: stop treating country choice like a vibe and start treating it like risk management. Right now, the U.S. is a higher-variance option for Nigerians because policy can block even the best application from turning into an actual arrival. Due to funding cuts, there have been reports of shrinking graduate cohorts, and funding uncertainty means some departments are admitting fewer graduate students, and international graduate enrollment has shown signs of decline. So the strategy is not “give up on the U.S.”, it is “build a portfolio”: pick 2 to 3 primary countries where (1) funding is structurally built into the degree, (2) visa pathways are currently predictable for you, and (3) your program fit is strong enough that you can compete; then keep the U.S. as a secondary track you pursue only if you can confirm your personal eligibility under the current rules (including whether you can get a valid visa or qualify under an exception), or if the program is open to deferral so you do not waste a full cycle. In practical terms, I tell people to spread applications across different systems, not just different schools: one bucket where you must secure a supervisor first (common in some countries and research routes), another bucket where admission can come before supervisor matching, and a third bucket of public scholarships that are not tied to one university; that way, a policy shift in one country does not cancel your entire future, it simply reroutes you to the options you already prepared.  

As you build that shortlist, write your academic CV. This is not just a document; it’s your evidence file. Your CV should make it easy for someone to see your trajectory: education, research or project experience, skills, awards, leadership, volunteering, publications or presentations if you have them. Do not wait until the week you want to apply. Start early and keep refining it as you gather more experiences. Even if you feel you don’t have much, you do. The difference is how you present it. And remember what I said in the first post: your story matters, but it must be backed by results, growth, and direction.  

Once your CV and shortlist are solid, then you earn the right to start approaching professors, but with sense. The best way to approach potential supervisors or professors is through what is called a “Cold Email”. Cold emails are not spam; they are professional introductions, and they matter more in certain locations and routes. In places like Australia, Canada, and Japan, securing a potential supervisor can be a major part of the application path, so you must treat it seriously. Your email should be short, respectful, and specific: who you are, what you’re interested in, why you chose them, and what you’re asking for (usually a short meeting or whether they are accepting students). Attach your CV. And don’t be discouraged by silence. Professors are busy. Follow up once, then keep moving. The goal is not to chase one person. The goal is to create options.  

Now let me address a painful reality people don’t like to hear: not everyone’s academic background is treated equally across systems, and you have to play smart, not emotional. If you have an HND or a second-class lower (2:2), credential evaluation can be a practical move for some schools and countries, because it translates your qualifications into a format their admissions committees understand. This doesn’t magically change your grades, but it can prevent your credentials from being misread or undervalued. Think of it as reducing friction, not begging for pity. So, if you graduated with a second-class lower or third class, my recommendation is that you should do a course-by-course evaluation with bodies such as the World Education Services (WES).  

Now, and only now, we get to the writing that can make or break everything: Statement of Purpose (SOP), motivation letter, personal statement, research statement, whichever your school requests. This is where you stop sounding like someone who “just wants to travel” and start sounding like someone who belongs in graduate school. Your statement is your argument. It connects your past experiences to your current interests and your future goals. It shows fit: why this program, why this school, why now. It shows readiness: what you’ve done that proves you can handle the work. And it shows direction: where you’re going and why it matters. This document is not something you rush in two nights. It is something you draft, refine, get feedback on, and tighten until it sounds like a confident human being with a plan. I will talk more about this in the near future.  

Finally, apply widely and apply intelligently. Apply to public scholarships, apply to multiple schools, and don’t put your future in the hands of one opportunity. This is where consistency beats talent. Some people apply once and start praying. Others apply ten times, improve each round, and eventually win. Also remember the supervisor rule: some schools require you to secure a supervisor before you apply, while others, especially many programs in the USA, do not. So your job is simple: know the rules for each program and meet every requirement with a competitive package. Do not submit half-prepared work and then blame “village people” when it doesn’t work.  

Let me end the same way I ended the last post, but even more practically: you do not need to be perfect to start, but you must be serious. This checklist is not meant to pressure you. It’s meant to guide you. Take one step this week. Get your passport. Request your transcript. Reach out to one to three lecturers or professors. Draft your CV. Build your shortlist. Write the first ugly draft of your statement because scholarships don’t reward wishful thinking. They reward people who build quietly, consistently, and strategically until the results have no choice but to show up.  

All the best!
