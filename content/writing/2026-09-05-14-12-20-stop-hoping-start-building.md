Most people lose the admission and/or scholarship before they ever press “submit,” and they do not even know it. It happens at the stage where you are choosing schools and programs. If you pick the wrong targets, you will spend months writing documents that cannot land, not because you are not good enough, but because you are applying in places that were never built to take you seriously or fund someone like you.  
  
Let me start from the most basic truth people skip: you cannot pick a graduate program in isolation from your foundational or undergraduate training. Your first degree is your foundation. It determines what you are already qualified for, what you can switch into easily, and what will require extra proof.  
For example, I studied Pharmacy. By my third year, I already knew I wanted drug discovery. Drug discovery is broad, but Medicinal Chemistry is one of the cleanest graduate paths inside that space, because it sits right at the point where chemistry, biology, and real drug development decisions meet. So my selection was not random. I did not wake up and just choose a fancy-sounding program. I asked myself, “What training does my Pharmacy background already support, and what graduate training will move me closer to drug discovery work?” That is how you pick, like a strategist.  
  
Let me break it down in a way that makes sense, especially if you are new and you are coming from a system where people try to confuse you with big grammar or use “agents” to sell you shortcuts. Choosing programs is like choosing a route. If you enter the wrong bus, you can sit comfortably, but you are still going to the wrong destination. So before you even open a university website, write two things on paper:  
  
1. what you want to study (even if it is broad), and  
2. what kind of training you want at the end.  
  
Example: “I want a Master’s in Data Science that leads to industry work,” or “I want a PhD where I can do research in cancer biology.” If you cannot say this clearly, you will keep applying randomly, and your SOP will sound confused too.  
  
Now here is the strategic part: When searching for a program that will fit your background properly, do not start with school names. Start with keywords. On Google, use the program keywords you are interested in (you can also use research interests), and then include the program level (masters or PhD). You can also incorporate the region or country of most interest to you. This is how you build a long list that is based on direction, not hype. Your first list should be 15 to 25 programs. Do not panic. It is not your final list. It is just raw material. Remember that you can modify your search keywords depending on your specific interests.  
  
After that, filter with three hard questions. This is the part that makes you a strategist.  
  
First filter: Fit. Does this program in that school genuinely train what I want, or am I forcing it? If you are aiming for research, can you name at least two faculty members or professors whose work connects to your interests? If you cannot find people doing what you claim you want to do, that is a sign you are in the wrong place, no matter how fine the website looks.  
  
Second filter: Funding. This is where many Nigerians waste time because some people assume “admission” equals “funding.” It does not. Ask a simple question early: is this degree normally funded for international students, and how? If it is a PhD, does the department commonly support students through assistantships, fellowships, or lab funding, or is it a “come and pay” situation? If it is a Master’s, is there a scholarship route, or are you expected to bring money? Do not let excitement blind you. If funding is not clear, treat it as risky until you confirm.  
  
Third filter: Feasibility. Can you realistically meet their requirements and timelines without suffering? This includes tests, transcripts, credential evaluation, application fees, deadlines, and document style. Some programs are friendly to waivers; some are strict. Some schools will process your application quickly; others will drag it. This is why I keep saying study abroad is a project. Your timeline is part of your competitiveness.  
  
Here is another thing beginners do not understand: countries and programs have different “admission styles.” And if you do not know the style, you will play the game wrongly.  
  
Bucket A: Supervisor first routes. In these routes, you often need to connect with a professor who is willing to take you before anything moves smoothly. Your strategy here is strong cold emails (I will definitely write more about this), good targeting, and patience.  
  
You usually do best when you identify a professor and reach out early.  
  
• Australia and New Zealand: often strongly advisor/project driven. Many applicants secure a supervisor’s interest before the application becomes serious.  
• Canada: it depends on the program, but in many research-based routes, a supervisor and funding fit matters early. Reaching out is often wise, especially for PhD.  
• Japan: commonly professor-lab centered. A professor’s willingness to host you can matter a lot.  
• Some parts of Asia (varies): in many cases, your lab match matters early even if there is a central application.  
  
Bucket B: Program first routes. In these routes, you apply to the department, get admitted, and then you match supervisors later. Your strategy here is a strong SOP, clear fit, and a clean application package.  
  
An example here is:  
  
• United States: many programs allow you to apply without a confirmed supervisor. Some do rotations in year 1, then you join a lab. Others admit you directly to a lab, so emailing faculty can still help, but it is not always required upfront.  
  
Bucket C: Scholarship first routes. In these routes, you win a government or external scholarship, and that scholarship becomes your funding engine. Your strategy here is aligning your story to the scholarship mission, not just the university.  
  
In some countries, you can apply to a specific funded PhD opening, and the supervisor is already part of the role.  
  
• Many places in continental Europe: a lot of PhD opportunities are advertised as funded positions tied to a specific project and PI. In that setup, you are not applying broadly to “a PhD”; you are applying to “this project with this group.”  
  
When you build your shortlist, do not put all your eggs in one bucket. Mix them. That is how you protect yourself from one system failing. This is what I mean by strategy. It is not fear. It is planning.  
  
Let me give you a very practical move that makes this feel less confusing. Open a simple spreadsheet and create columns like: Program, Country, Degree, Deadline, Test needed, Funding path, Supervisor needed (Yes or No), Fees, Notes on fit, and Score. Then score each program from 1 to 5 on Fit, Funding, and Feasibility. If a school is scoring low on funding clarity, you do not “pray it works.” You either confirm, or you drop it. This is how serious people save time.  
  
And please hear me on this: do not allow anybody to turn this process into a marketplace where they are selling you secrets. Nobody should collect money to “help you choose schools” without teaching you how to verify information yourself. The university website, the department pages, and the faculty pages are usually enough to build a smart shortlist. Mentors can guide you, yes. But you should still know what you are doing.  
  
If you want a clear next step after reading this, do this in one week: build a long list of 20 programs from different countries using keywords, filter it down to 10 using fit and funding clarity, then cut it to 6 to 8 serious targets using feasibility and deadlines. When you have that, you are no longer “interested in studying abroad.” You have started building.
