Mentorship · Part 1

Stop Hoping. Start Building.

How does getting a fully funded scholarship actually happen?

By Raphael S. OnukuSeptember 5, 20265 minute read

Mentorship

← All writing

Let me say this plainly, with my full chest. Fully funded Master’s and PhD opportunities abroad are real. They are not rumors, not a “special people” thing, and not something reserved for geniuses or people with connections. I have lived it, I am living it, and I have watched others do it too. The reason many people never get close is not because they are not good enough. It is because they do not know the game exists, so they never learn the rules. Once you understand that scholarships are awarded through evidence, fit, and strategy, the whole journey stops looking like magic and starts looking like a process.

Here is the mindset shift that changed my life. I became convinced that studying abroad is not a wish. It is a project. When I became serious in 2020 during my NYSC, I stopped “hoping” and started building. I tracked deadlines like a professional. I treated my CV like a proof document, not a biography. I learned that your personal story matters, but it must be backed by results, growth, and direction. That approach helped me earn a fully funded scholarship in Taiwan through the Taiwan Government Ministry of Education, and it carried me forward into my current PhD training in the United States. The win did not come because I was lucky. It came because I learned how to package my value, show my readiness, and apply with intention.

Most people think scholarships are only about grades. Grades help, yes, but scholarships are more about signals. Reviewers want to see three things. They want evidence that you can handle the program, that you match what they fund, and that you will use the opportunity well. That is why strategy matters. You do not randomly apply everywhere. You pick countries, schools, and labs that actually fund your type of applicant. You build a shortlist based on fit and funding history. You write an SOP that sounds like a future researcher. You choose recommenders who can tell real stories about you, not just repeat your title. And you apply early enough to revise, rather than waiting until panic sets in.

But I also need to be honest with you. Graduate school is not for everybody. Not because some people are less intelligent, but because it demands a certain kind of patience, focus, and tolerance for uncertainty. If you hate long projects, if you cannot commit to learning deeply, or if you just want the “abroad” life without the work that comes with it, then graduate school will frustrate you. There is no shame in choosing another path. The goal is not to impress people. The goal is to choose what fits your values and your future. For the person who truly wants it, though, graduate school can be a launchpad that reshapes your skills, confidence, network, and earning power.

If you are still doubting yourself, let me give you something practical to hold. You do not need to be perfect to start, but you must be serious. Start by gathering your documents and cleaning your timeline. Build a strong, simple CV. Write one clear paragraph about what you want to study and why. Identify your programs of interest, then study their requirements like a blueprint. Reach out to mentors, not for motivation, but for feedback. Most importantly, stop telling yourself that “people like me do not get these things.” People like you get them every year, but those who win are usually those who learn, prepare, and apply strategically. That is why someone with a third-class degree may secure full funding while a first-class graduate may not.
